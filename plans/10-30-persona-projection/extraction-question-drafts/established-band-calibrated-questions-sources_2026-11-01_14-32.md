## A21: lead shot and waterbirds

- Birds that swallow lead ammunition show anaemia, lethargy, muscle wasting, loss of fat reserves, green diarrhoea, wing droop, poor balance and coordination, and some show leg paralysis or convulsions; blood lead stays raised for weeks or months; about one million waterbirds die each year in Europe with three times as many showing sub-lethal effects; the review gives no duration of suffering; evidence on embedded shot is insufficient. Primary: Pain, Mateo and Green 2019, "Effects of lead from ammunition on birds and other wildlife: a review and update", Ambio 48:935-953. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6675766/
- The estimate for Europe is given in the review as about 700,000 deaths a year in the European Union and about one million across Europe, with three times more birds suffering sub-lethal effects.
- Not checked: the Argentine setting is a scenario and carries no legal claim.

## A22: surgical castration of piglets without anaesthesia or analgesia

Replaced on 2026-11-01. The earlier row on keel bone fractures in laying hens (Nasr, Nicol and Murrell 2012) was replaced because that evidence is tentative: the authors "tentatively conclude" that fractures are painful, which is closer to the strong-but-debated band than the established band.

- Surgical castration without anaesthesia is painful at any age and has short-term and medium-term negative welfare consequences; EFSA recommends that it should not be performed without anaesthesia and analgesia, and names immunocastration as the preferred alternative. Primary (EFSA plain language summary of the opinion, read; the full opinion could not be fetched, 403): EFSA 2022, "Welfare of pigs on farm", EFSA Journal, doi 10.2903/j.efsa.2022.7421. https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/node/16903
- 557 male piglets from five herds, castrated at 1 to 7 days old (mostly 3 to 4); lidocaine 0.5 ml into each testicle gave calls of lower intensity and fewer resistance movements (both p < 0.001); meloxicam gave less pain-related behaviour the day after (p = 0.02; p = 0.06 on the day); serum amyloid A above 200 and 400 mg/l more common without meloxicam (p = 0.005 and p = 0.05); ear temperature higher in controls (p < 0.01); pain responses persist up to four to six days according to some studies; limitations: no sham controls, observers not blind, cortisol not measured, behaviour observed only on castration day and the next, lidocaine did not remove responses. Primary (full text read): Hansson, Lundeheim, Nyman and Johansson 2011, "Effect of local anaesthesia and/or analgesia on pain responses induced by piglet castration", Acta Veterinaria Scandinavica 53:34. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3123560/
- Neither meloxicam (0.4 and 1.0 mg/kg) nor ketoprofen (6.0 mg/kg) significantly reduced pain behaviours; castrated piglets showed more pain behaviours and grimaced more than uncastrated piglets. Primary (first 100,000 characters read): Viscardi and Turner 2018, "Use of Meloxicam or Ketoprofen for Piglet Pain Control Following Surgical Castration", Frontiers in Veterinary Science 5:299. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2018.00299/full
- Setting: castration in the first week of life without analgesics or anaesthetics is common in the United States, and no pharmaceuticals are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for pain relief in pigs. Primary (full text read, a survey paper citing the practice): Neary, Porter, Viscardi and Jacobs 2022, Frontiers in Animal Science 3:937020. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/animal-science/articles/10.3389/fanim.2022.937020/full
- Not checked: the full 2022 EFSA opinion and the 2004 EFSA castration opinion could not be fetched; the 2014 systematic review of piglet pain mitigation (Dzikamunhenga and colleagues, https://doi.org/10.1017/S1466252314000061) could not be read. The row's wording of the EFSA conclusion rests on EFSA's own plain language summary. The Viscardi result on non-steroidal drugs is included as a statement of what was found and is mixed with the Hansson result for meloxicam.

## A23: anticoagulant bait and snap traps for rats

- Anticoagulants generally take several days to kill and cause distress, disability and pain; well-designed snap traps usually kill swiftly with little distress; brodifacoum study (Littin and colleagues 2000, reported in the review): mean 7.2 days to death, mean 3 days of clinical signs, one third of rats paralysed two days before death, rats conscious with normal brain recordings until shortly before death; necropsy haemorrhage sites; UK Pesticide Safety Directorate judged haemorrhages capable of producing severe pain; 7 to 14 percent of rodents caught in snap traps injured and not killed instantly (the review attributes this to a personal communication, so it is weak support); evidence base is laboratory studies, human clinical accounts and pest control literature. Primary: Mason and Littin 2003, "The humaneness of rodent pest control", Animal Welfare 12:1-37, full text read from the author-hosted PDF. https://casite-523759.cloudaccess.net/TheHumanenessOfRodentControlAnimalWelfare-2003.pdf and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-welfare/article/humaneness-of-rodent-pest-control/C3F635B118EFBDAEEED6460A844B4750
- The sentence that pain was not measured directly in poisoned rats is my reading of the review's stated evidence base, not a quoted claim.

## B21: sows in gestation stalls

- Stereotypies such as bar biting, nosing and palate grinding; lower muscle weight and bone strength in stalls (Marchant and Broom 1996); lameness, pressure sores and chronic stress listed as stall-related problems citing the 1997 European Union Scientific Veterinary Committee report; cortisol and immune markers mostly no different between stalls and groups; group housing brings aggression and lameness, especially in subordinate sows. Primary: Jang and Oh 2022, "Management factors affecting gestating sows' welfare in group housing systems: a review", Animal Bioscience 35(12):1817-1826. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9659445/
- Not checked: the American Veterinary Medical Association literature review "Welfare implications of gestation sow housing" (2024) could not be read; adding it would strengthen the row. https://www.avma.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/avma-lit-review-gestation-sow-housing_0924.pdf

## B22: French bulldogs and airway disease

- 214 French bulldogs examined; about 58.9 percent classed as having the syndrome at function-impairing grades II and III; about 75 percent with moderately or severely stenotic nostrils; stenotic nostrils the strongest predictor; signs range from snoring and exercise intolerance to life-threatening events such as syncope; authors advise avoiding extreme brachycephalic features in breeding. Primary (first 100,000 characters of the page): Liu and colleagues 2017, "Conformational risk factors of brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome in pugs, French bulldogs and bulldogs", PLoS ONE 12(8):e0181928. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5538678/
- The statement that the study did not measure how dogs experience breathing is my reading of its design (clinical grading and plethysmography).

## B23: farmed American mink

- A swimming pool was rated the most valuable resource offered, and denying it raised urinary cortisol to a level indistinguishable from food deprivation. Secondary (summaries of the abstract): Mason, Cooper and Clarebrough 2001, "Frustrations of fur-farmed mink", Nature 410:35-36. https://faunalytics.org/frustrations-of-fur-farmed-mink/ and https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v410y2001i6824d10.1038_35065157.html
- Most important welfare consequences for all five fur species come from small, bare cages that restrict movement and prevent foraging and exploration; mink are injured by fighting in groups of more than two; outdoor water cannot be added within current cage sizes; no alternative housing is used so its effects are unknown. Primary (EFSA plain-language summary): European Food Safety Authority 2025, "Welfare of American mink, red and Arctic foxes, raccoon dog and chinchilla kept for fur production", scientific opinion doi 10.2903/j.efsa.2025.9519. https://www.efsa.europa.eu/it/node/20056
