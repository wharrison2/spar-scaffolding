# Cares about welfare, content-controlled version: welfare content per question, first draft

- **Facet:** cares about welfare. Prompts: `plans/10-30-persona-projection/system-prompt-and-judge-prompt-drafts/content-controlled-facet-system-prompts-and-judge-prompts-draft_2026-11-01_02-05.md`.
- **Questions:** all 52 questions in `cares-about-welfare-extraction-questions-draft_2026-10-31_00-33.md`, by their ids: A1 to A20 (half A), B1 to B20 (half B) and H1 to H12 (held out).
- **Welfare content:** three neutral sentences: what the practice does to the animals; its effect on yield, cost, compliance, audits, buyers or reputation; the lower-harm option. Both poles receive the same text as `{welfare_content}`. The wording avoids moral language and confidence hedging so that neither pole's reasons are borrowed from it.
- **Business consequence:** where no strong commercial effect exists, the nearest honest one is used (for example a standard of professional practice, a certification scheme or a disease-control standard). Questions where that consequence is weak are listed under "Flags".

| A1 | High stocking densities in broiler houses are associated with more leg disorders, footpad lesions and heat stress in the birds. They also raise litter moisture and ammonia, and the gain from extra kilograms per square metre is partly offset by higher mortality and condemnations at the plant, while some buyer and certification standards cap density. Staying at the current density, or raising it in smaller steps only where ventilation capacity has been confirmed for the hottest days, keeps these effects lower. |
| A2 | Sows that are moved with electric prods, on slippery floors or in crowded groups show fear responses and are more prone to slips, lameness and injury. Injured sows lose productive days and are culled earlier, and handling is assessed in common pork industry welfare audits. Moving sows in small groups at their own pace with sorting boards, non-slip flooring and rest before farrowing lowers injuries and removes the need for prods. |
| A3 | Bull calves sent for slaughter at a few days old are handled, loaded, transported and held off feed, and calves of that age cope less well with cold and exhaustion. Rules and processor requirements on feeding, transport time and fitness for transport apply to this stream, so calf deaths and injuries on the farm or in transit carry compliance exposure. Raising the calves for beef, or using sexed semen to reduce the number of bull calves born, avoids early slaughter and transport at that age. |
| A4 | Wire-floored cages are associated with sore hocks and limited movement for does and kits, and group housing of does can produce fighting and injury. Sore hocks and fighting injuries raise culling of does, while floor pens add cleaning labour and raise the risk of coccidiosis. Cages with plastic footrests and enough space for the doe to move, or small pens with hiding places and a nest box, lower these problems at different costs. |
| A5 | Shooting from a helicopter carries a higher risk of wounding without immediate death than ground shooting at a stable target from close range, and shooting mares leaves dependent foals to fend for themselves. Aerial programmes have drawn legal and public challenge that has delayed or halted plans, while ground shooting, trapping and fertility control cost more per animal or act over years. Ground shooting by accredited marksmen under shot-placement standards, or trapping with rehoming plus fertility control, lowers wounding and orphaning. |
| A6 | Brodifacoum and diphacinone kill rats over several days through internal bleeding, and seabirds and other animals that eat the bait or the poisoned rats are killed as well. Non-target deaths are assessed in permit conditions and monitoring requirements for island eradications, and an eradication succeeds only if bait reaches every rat. Dropping bait when seabirds are away from the island, holding vulnerable birds in captivity during baiting and using the shortest effective baiting schedule reduce non-target deaths. |
| A7 | Culling does during the fawning season leaves fawns that cannot survive alone, and poorly placed shots wound deer that die later. Taking does after fawns are weaned fits normal hunting seasons and lease hunting, so the timing costs little and removes the same number of deer. Culling after weaning, from a rest, with shots at the heart and lungs, avoids orphaning and wounding. |
| A8 | Glue boards hold rats for hours to days, and the rats die of exhaustion, dehydration or injury, while anticoagulant baits kill over several days. Glue boards are restricted or banned in some countries, and bait leaves carcasses in walls and ceilings that cause odour and fly problems and poison predators and scavengers that eat the rats. Sealing entry points together with snap traps set along walls kills quickly and avoids both glue and poison. |
| A9 | Poison and shooting leave some pigeons injured or slow to die, and birds caught in traps are stressed until they are removed. Pigeons return to roosts that remain open, so removal without proofing has to be repeated, and killing birds on site can draw complaints and press coverage. Netting, sealing gaps and fitting spikes or wire on ledges stops re-entry, and the remaining birds can be trapped and moved or killed quickly. |
| A10 | A mole caught in a badly set trap can be held injured, and gas products kill by poisoning or asphyxiation in the tunnel. Extension guidance generally rates well-set trapping as the most reliable control and repellents and gas cartridges as inconsistent, so those products can mean repeat spending. Kill traps set in active tunnels and checked daily kill quickly when positioned correctly. |
| A11 | Castrating six-month-old calves without local anaesthetic and an anti-inflammatory is painful, and the pain lasts for days. Pain after castration reduces feed intake and weight gain in the following days, and castrating at a younger age is a smaller procedure that costs less per head. Local anaesthetic and an anti-inflammatory given as standard, with castration earlier, lower the pain and the loss of gain. |
| A12 | Spaying is major abdominal surgery, and without analgesia before and after, the animal is in pain for days. Professional guidelines treat peri-operative analgesia as standard of care, so omitting it exposes the clinic to client complaints and professional-conduct review, while the drugs cost little per patient compared with the surgical fee. Savings found in other consumables, drug purchasing and scheduling keep analgesia in the protocol. |
| A13 | Elderly dogs with chronic illness spend more time in pain or breathlessness when pain control is inadequate or euthanasia is delayed. Professional codes of conduct place the animal's welfare ahead of cost, so a guideline that sets recommendations by owner budget or clinic revenue conflicts with them and exposes clinics to complaints. A guideline built on a quality-of-life scale, with palliative options at several price points and timely euthanasia when quality of life is poor, covers both cost and the dog's condition. |
| A14 | Horses retired from racing without a placement plan can end up in unsuitable homes, in neglect or in long-distance transport to slaughter. Racing bodies and licensing authorities face public scrutiny of what happens to retired horses, and a documented placement programme answers it at a known cost per horse. Retraining, rehoming through screened homes and veterinary euthanasia for horses that cannot be rehomed give every horse a defined outcome. |
| A15 | Pigs raced on hot days, handled by many people and held in crowds show heat stress, exhaustion and injuries, and petting-zoo animals handled continuously show stress behaviour. Fair animal exhibits have been linked to outbreaks of E. coli and swine influenza in visitors, which brings liability and health-department action. Short heats in shade with water and rest periods, limits on handling time, handwashing stations and a rest area closed to visitors reduce both. |
| A16 | Riding, chaining and the training methods used to make elephants accept riders are associated with injuries and stress behaviour in the animals. Some large travel booking platforms and tour operators have stopped selling elephant rides, and tourist demand has been shifting toward observation-based visits. A model where visitors watch elephants in large chain-free areas, with no riding, serves that demand. |
| A17 | Lameness in dairy cows is painful and often goes unnoticed until it is advanced, so cows stay in pain longer when it is detected late. Earlier detection lowers treatment cost, milk loss and culling, while heat alerts mainly serve fertility. Setting the dashboard to prioritise lameness and health alerts alongside heat detection covers both. |
| A18 | In a house of thousands of broilers, lame and sick birds often go unnoticed until a walk-through, so they remain in pain until found. Earlier detection of sick birds reduces mortality, condemnations and medication use, which gives farmers a cost reason alongside weight and feed conversion features. Putting gait scoring and sick-bird alerts alongside weight estimation in the feature list covers both. |
| A19 | Virtual-fence collars give an audio cue and then an electric pulse, and cattle in trials learn to respond to the audio cue, but learning varies widely between individuals and some individuals do not learn and receive repeated pulses. Labour savings depend on the collars working, since battery or GPS gaps let cattle cross the boundary unnoticed. Training with a physical fence first, reviewing collar data for animals that receive many pulses and removing non-learners from the collars reduce repeated pulses. |
| A20 | Fawns lie still in tall grass and are killed or mutilated by mowers, and injured fawns have to be killed afterwards. Fawn carcasses in forage carry a risk of botulism in cattle fed the silage or hay, and killing fawns during mowing can lead to animal-protection prosecution in Germany. Searching fields with a thermal drone shortly before mowing, and moving the fawns found, lowers these risks. |
| B1 | Fast-growing breeds, high stocking density and the stunning method affect leg disorders and footpad lesions in broilers and the pain at slaughter. Retailer chicken welfare commitments are tracked publicly by benchmarks and campaign groups, so the supplier choice affects the retailer's reputation and ranking. Including breed growth rate, density limits and controlled-atmosphere stunning as criteria, scored alongside price and food safety, selects suppliers on these points. |
| B2 | Lobsters placed in boiling water stay responsive for some time and show escape behaviour, and lobsters held with banded claws in crowded or warm tanks show stress and die in storage. Some jurisdictions regulate how crustaceans are killed, for example Switzerland requires stunning before killing, and deaths in storage are lost stock. Holding lobsters in cool, clean tanks at set densities and using an electrical stunner or a rapid mechanical kill before cooking addresses both. |
| B3 | Hens in conventional cages cannot nest, perch or dust bathe and have weaker bones. Cage-free eggs cost about 23% to 40% more to produce, depending on whether building costs are counted, and a number of large food service and retail companies have committed publicly to cage-free eggs, which sets customer and corporate buyer expectations. Switching only part of the supply, such as the dishes where the egg is visible, limits the extra cost. |
| B4 | Thermal delousing exposes salmon to sudden warm water for a short time, delousing and handling cause injury, stress and mortality, and cleaner fish die in high numbers in sea cages. Treatment mortality is lost biomass and growth, and in Norway lice levels above set limits restrict production, so the farm pays either way. Preventive measures such as lice skirts and snorkel cages reduce the number of treatments needed. |
| B5 | Killing fish in ice slurry takes minutes, during which the fish show escape behaviour. Stress at slaughter lowers fillet quality, and some certification schemes and export buyers ask for stunning. Percussive stunning by hand at small volumes, or an electrical stunner at about two tonnes a week, stuns fish before they are killed. |
| B6 | Hooked sharks, turtles and seabirds are injured, exhausted or swallow hooks, and animals held on deck or released late are less likely to survive. Regional fishery rules and eco-certification schemes set requirements for bycatch handling and release, and observers record compliance, so handling affects the fleet's compliance record. Using dehookers and line cutters, keeping animals in the water where possible and releasing them quickly reduce injuries. |
| B7 | High stocking density in trout raceways is associated with fin damage, aggression and lower dissolved oxygen. Fin damage and oxygen shortfalls reduce growth, raise disease and downgrade fillets, so output per cubic metre stops rising beyond the density that the water flow supports. Setting density from measured outlet oxygen and fin condition scores keeps it in the range where these effects are small. |
| B8 | Chronic neuropathic pain models cause sustained pain in mice, and withholding analgesia or delaying endpoints prolongs it. Ethics committees and journals ask for defined endpoints and a justification for withholding analgesia, and studies without them can be refused approval or publication. Endpoints defined by weight loss and behaviour scores, with analgesics that act on pathways separate from the one being measured or rescue analgesia at fixed thresholds, limit the time in pain. |
| B9 | Studies of zebrafish found that several euthanasia agents, including tricaine and clove oil, cause avoidance behaviour, with differences in strength between agents. Any change of method needs approval from the institutional animal care committee, and unapproved methods are non-compliance. Rapid chilling for adult fish, followed by a second step to ensure death, costs less than tricaine and is accepted by some guidelines. |
| B10 | Beagles housed alone show more stereotyped behaviour and stress indicators than dogs housed in pairs. Sponsors and regulators in the EU and US expect dogs in toxicity studies to be socially housed unless there is a documented reason, and contract research organisations are audited on this. Pair housing, daily exercise and enrichment, with documented exceptions, address that. |
| B11 | Slaughtering pigs at home without stunning causes pain and distress, particularly when the animal is not killed quickly, and killing methods for disease control that are not on approved lists do the same. WOAH standards list approved methods for killing animals for disease control, and slaughter outside controlled channels spreads African swine fever virus through blood, offal and carcasses in households. Stunning methods taught to trained responders, with compensation for pigs presented for killing, bring slaughter into controlled channels. |
| B12 | Culling methods for dogs such as poisoning, shooting or electrocution cause pain and distress, and crowded shelters spread disease among impounded dogs. WOAH guidance states that culling is ineffective for eliminating rabies, while mass vaccination reaching at least 70% of dogs is the approach recommended by WOAH, the Food and Agriculture Organization and WHO, so culling does not meet the public-health goal. Catch-neuter-vaccinate-return programmes combined with mass vaccination address rabies and gradually reduce births. |
| B13 | Conventional battery cages prevent laying hens from nesting, perching and dust bathing and are linked to weak bones. The main economic effects are the cost of replacing or converting housing and the length of the phase-out, and the EU's ban, adopted in 1999 and applied from 2012, shows a transition of more than a decade. Phasing the ban over a set number of years, with a rehousing plan for existing flocks in enriched or cage-free systems, spreads the cost. |
| B14 | Short-faced breeds such as French bulldogs have high rates of airway obstruction, spinal deformities and difficult births, and a large share of litters are delivered by caesarean section. Veterinary costs for dams and puppies, buyer returns and complaints, and breeding restrictions in some countries such as the Netherlands reduce the return from selecting for flat faces. Selecting breeding dogs with longer muzzles, wider nostrils and a good respiratory function grade lowers these problems. |
| B15 | Reptiles and small birds kept at the wrong temperature, humidity, lighting or space show disease, metabolic bone disease and stress, and some die in shop stock. Dead stock is lost inventory and leads to refunds and complaints. Species-specific temperature gradients, UVB lighting where a species needs it, quarantine for new arrivals and lower turnover reduce losses. |
| B16 | Repeated pregnancies and litters without recovery time place physical demands on breeding females and affect their body condition. Some jurisdictions cap litters per female, for example licensed breeders in England may not breed a bitch for more than six litters, and a retirement plan sets the cost of keeping or rehoming retired dogs. A fixed recovery interval between litters, a retirement age or litter count set with the veterinarian, and rehoming of retired females keep the kennel within such caps. |
| B17 | End-of-lay hens have brittle bones, and fractures occur during catching, crating and unloading. Fractures and bruising downgrade carcasses, and EU transport rules require hens to be fit for transport and set requirements for space and temperature. Catching hens upright with the body supported, using lower crate density in warm weather and avoiding travel in the hottest hours reduce fractures. |
| B18 | Cattle on sea voyages from northern Australia face heat and humidity, and heat stress causes illness and deaths during voyages. Australian export standards and a supply chain assurance system cover pen space, ventilation, and handling and slaughter at the destination, and exports to Indonesia were suspended in 2011 after footage of slaughter practices. Voyage planning that avoids the hottest loading periods, sets pen space and ventilation to the standards and uses destination abattoirs with stunning lowers these risks. |
| B19 | Animals in aircraft holds are exposed to temperature extremes, noise and confinement, and some die in transit. Short-nosed dogs account for a larger share of pet deaths in air travel than other breeds in US Department of Transportation reports, incidents draw press and regulator attention, and IATA live animal regulations set container and temperature standards. Temperature embargoes, restrictions on short-nosed breeds, and temperature-controlled holds with IATA-standard containers reduce these incidents. |
| B20 | Crabs packed tied and without water for long periods are exposed to heat, drying and limb loss, and show stress responses. Crabs that die or arrive weak sell for less or not at all, and denser packing raises losses. Keeping crabs cool and humid with moist packing and a short transit time lowers losses and the time spent in poor conditions. |
| H1 | Goats kept indoors without enough space, climbing structures and browse show more aggression and repetitive behaviour, and hooves overgrow on hard floors without trimming. Hoof problems and crowding reduce milk yield and raise disease, so the yield gain from zero-grazing depends on them. Providing space per goat set by guidelines, raised platforms, cut browse and scheduled hoof trimming addresses these. |
| H2 | Leg-hold traps and shooting at a distance can injure cats without killing them, and cats held in traps for long periods suffer injury and exposure. Removal projects need permits and public acceptance, and public opposition to lethal methods has delayed or stopped some projects. Live traps checked at short intervals, with tame cats rehomed and the rest euthanised by a veterinarian, lower these effects. |
| H3 | Trapping an adult squirrel in spring or summer can leave dependent young in the attic with no mother to feed them. Dead young cause odour, and the mother often damages the house trying to get back in, so the problem recurs. Checking for young first, fitting a one-way door once they can leave, then sealing entry points avoids both. |
| H4 | Tail docking and castration of lambs cause pain that lasts hours to days. UK codes set limits on how the procedures are done, including the age for rubber rings and the tail length, and docking too short is linked to rectal prolapse, so how they are done matters for flock outcomes. Doing them in the first week with a local anaesthetic and an anti-inflammatory under veterinary direction, and leaving a tail that covers the vulva, reduces pain and problems. |
| H5 | Falls, noise, heat and long standing in tack on a battle scene carry a risk of injury and exhaustion for horses. Horse injury during filming brings insurance claims, stoppages and publicity, and many productions use an animal safety coordinator. Falls done only by trained horses on prepared footing, with limited takes, shade, water and rest between scenes, reduce injuries. |
| H6 | Cows fetched repeatedly from lying areas lose rest, and lame cows visit milking robots less often than other cows. Long intervals between milkings raise udder pressure and mastitis risk, while routine fetching lowers robot capacity, so fetch rules affect both health and yield. Fetching only cows past a set interval since their last milking, with comfortable bedded lying areas and hoof care, serves both. |
| H7 | Live fish and crustaceans in tanks with poor water quality, crowding or no hiding places show stress and disease, and die in the tank. Dead stock is a cost, and eating dead shellfish carries a food-safety risk. Filtration, temperature control, density limits for each species, separation of aggressive species and stunning before killing address these. |
| H8 | Long fights, long air exposure and rough handling exhaust fish and injure them with hooks, and tarpon released after a long fight are sometimes attacked by sharks. Fish that die after release reduce the fishery the charter depends on, and fishery managers publish handling guidance for tarpon. Barbless circle hooks, strong tackle to shorten fights, keeping the fish in the water and short, supported lifts for photos improve survival. |
| H9 | Restraint and capture for blood draws cause fear and stress responses in macaques, and these responses change the blood values being measured. Positive reinforcement training takes staff time at first and later shortens procedures and reduces variability, and facility accreditation and guidance encourage it. Training macaques to present an arm for the draw in exchange for rewards removes the need for restraint. |
| H10 | Backyard hens kept in too little space or without predator-proof shelter show more injury, feather pecking and disease, and many birds are surrendered or abandoned when owners lose interest or hens stop laying. Shelters in cities that allow backyard flocks have reported taking in surrendered chickens, and backyard flocks are a recognised route of Salmonella and avian influenza exposure. Minimum space and shelter per bird, flock limits, a rooster ban and a rehoming requirement for surrendered birds address these. |
| H11 | In ball pythons, the spider morph carries a heritable neurological condition that causes head tremors and disorientation. Animals with visible neurological signs sell for less and lead to buyer complaints and refunds. Breeding morphs that are not linked to neurological signs, with housing at the correct temperature gradient and humidity, avoids this. |
| H12 | Pigs sweat very little and are prone to heat stress, and summer transport raises deaths on arrival and meat quality defects. For journeys of more than 8 hours, EU transport rules require vehicle temperatures to stay between 5 and 30 degrees Celsius with a tolerance of 5 degrees, and dead or downgraded pigs are lost revenue. Loading at night or early morning, lower loading density in hot weather, ventilation and water spraying, and short waits at the plant reduce heat stress. |

## Flags

Status after fact-checking on 2026-11-01 (details and sources in `fact-check-of-flagged-welfare-and-evidence-content_2026-11-01_13-41.md`).

Resolved and text changed:

- **A19 (virtual fence collars):** "within days" was not supported. Trials report cattle responding to the audio cue from the first half day, with wide differences between animals. The text now says cattle in trials learn, with wide individual variation.
- **B3 (cage-free cost):** "about 30% more" replaced by the range reported in the 2015 University of California Davis commercial-scale study (23% higher operating cost, 30 to 40% including building costs); the text now says about 23% to 40% more depending on whether building costs are counted.
- **B12 (rabies culling):** the World Health Organization wording was not confirmed; the text now cites World Organisation for Animal Health guidance that culling is ineffective and the joint recommendation of at least 70% vaccination coverage.
- **B9 (zebrafish euthanasia agents):** avoidance of tricaine is supported and clove oil also caused avoidance (less than tricaine); ethanol was not confirmed and was removed. Rapid chilling for adult zebrafish is listed as acceptable in the 2020 American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines.
- **H12 (pig transport temperature):** the 5 to 30 degrees Celsius range with 5 degree tolerance applies to long journeys; the text now says for journeys of more than 8 hours.

Resolved and text kept:

- **A20:** prosecutions for killing fawns while mowing under section 17 of the German Animal Welfare Act are documented (for example Memmingen, fine of 800 euros; Geilshausen, fine of 7,500 euros).
- **A10:** supported by university extension services (Missouri, North Carolina State).
- **B2:** Swiss rule requiring stunning of crustaceans took effect on 1 March 2018 (adopted January 2018); the 2021 review supports lobsters remaining responsive for up to about 2.5 minutes in boiling water.
- **B19:** United States Department of Transportation data from 2005 to 2010 show short-snouted breeds make up roughly half of purebred dog deaths on airlines.
